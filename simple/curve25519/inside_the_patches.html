Inside the X25519 Patches

Goldmont N3350 · Celeron · pinned 1.1 GHz · 2026-09-14

Inside the X25519 Patches

What the implementation actually does, why each decision was made, and the case to put in front of a reviewer — including the parts that argue against us, which are the parts a referee will find first.

Part I

How the implementation works

Five layers, from the micro-op schedule up to the scalar multiplication. Each one exists because of a measured constraint, not a preference.

1 — The shape of it

Two microcode patches, one inline-assembly ladder

fe_mul patch58 triads, 174 µops. Five independent 128-bit accumulators. Hooked on vmwrite (0x0cd8).
fe_sq patch42 triads, 123 µops. Symmetry gives 15 products instead of 25. Hooked on vmread (0x0618).
wrapper macrosMarshal 10 limbs into fixed architectural registers, fire the hooked instruction, store 5 back.
ladder_stepOne asm volatile block per ladder iteration. RBP addresses the whole state; five register chains skip reloads.
fe_invertAll 254 squarings of the Fermat chain in one asm block, each run chained through registers.

100 of the 128 available triads are used. That budget is the binding constraint on everything in Part I — it is why there is no fused multi-operation patch, and it is the reason the 4×64 control loses.

2 — The mechanism

Per-destination carry state is the whole idea

On Goldmont, an arithmetic µop leaves condition state attached to its destination register. SETCC_CONDB materialises that carry as a 0 or 1 into an ordinary register. Five accumulators therefore run five carry chains that never interact.

Architectural x86 cannot do this. Every ADC reads the one carry flag in RFLAGS, so independent accumulation chains must be serialised or interleaved by hand — and this core has neither BMI2 nor ADX, so there is no MULX/ADCX/ADOX escape.

The evidence is a controlled A/B, and it is the strongest thing in the paper. A variant using the GENARITHFLAGS+ADC bridge is fewer operations — 3 per accumulate instead of 4, 52 triads instead of 58 — and it is slower: 104.0 against 102.8 cycles. It loses because all 20 bridged carries funnel through the single architectural CF and serialise, while five SETCC domains do not. Fewer ops, more time: that is the interface difference showing up as a number.

What one accumulate looks like

/* four µops, not three -- and the fourth is what makes it fast */
ADD   (lo_j,  lo_j,  lo_p)     // accumulate the product's low half
SETCC (c,     lo_j)            // materialise THIS accumulator's carry
ADD   (hi_p,  hi_p,  c)        // fold it into the product's high half...
ADD   (hi_j,  hi_j,  hi_p)     // ...not into hi_j, halving that chain: 4 deep, not 8

Folding the carry into the product's high half rather than the accumulator's is a small thing that halves a dependency chain. Do not "save" the fourth operation.

3 — fe_mul

Three tricks past the obvious schoolbook

Row 0 initialises for free

MUL writes its low half into srcB. So staging bj into loj and multiplying is the initialisation of accumulator j — no ADD and no SETCC for the first product of every limb. Five accumulators, five free initialisations.

19·bj without destroying bj

IMUL64L is a non-destructive 64-bit low multiply: both sources survive, unlike MUL. Four of them in PREP produce the reduced operands while b stays live for row 0.

Two fully parallel reduction passes

OpenSSL propagates each carry into its 128-bit accumulator — two instructions natively, but ADD+SETCC+ADD in microcode. Splitting every accumulator first instead:

r_j = lo_j & MASK51          q_j = (lo_j >> 51) | (hi_j << 13)

makes every subsequent carry a plain 64-bit ADD, and all five splits issue in parallel. The caller passes 251−1 in RCX so the mask is one AND instead of a SHL13/SHR13 pair — RCX was free, because MUL's destination register is unconstrained.

Input bound

Limbs must be below 254. The binding constraint is 19·q4 < 264 with q4 = acc4 ≫ 51 and acc4 < 5·22L, needing L < 54.2. The ladder feeds at most 253.1 — FE_SUB's 2p bias on top of a limb below 252.

The result is packed at exactly 3.0 µops per triad (174 / 58) with a dependency depth of 25.4 against a 48.6 issue floor — no longer dependency-bound, and with no slack to remove a triad without removing work.

4 — The calling convention

A 30-cycle stall that lives in the load order

The patch reads and writes fixed architectural registers, so the wrapper marshals limbs into place before the hooked instruction and stores results after:

fe_mul   a → rdi rsi r12 r11 r14      b → r15 r13 r9 r10 rbx    rcx = 2^51-1
         fire: vmwrite rcx, rdx
         out ← r15 r13 r9 r10 rax

fe_sq    a → rdi rsi r12 r11 r14   2a → r15 r13 r9 r10   19·a4 → rbx   19·a3 → rdx
         fire: .byte 0x0f, 0x78, 0xca   (vmread rdx, rcx)
         out ← rdi r9 r10 rbx rax

The five loads must run limb 0 → 4. They once ran 4 → 0. Stores are ascending, so a descending load order makes the first load of one operation read [x+32] one instruction after the previous operation's last store wrote it. That store-to-load distance of 1 costs about 30 cycles on Goldmont. Ascending puts each load five instructions after its producing store and the stall disappears: 122.3 → 81.8 cycles per dependent fe_sq, with the patch untouched. Do not tidy these back into descending order.

A firing costs 16.2 cycles before any arithmetic — measured by firing the real wrapper at a one-triad no-op patch. That number carries most of Part II.

5 — The ladder

One assembly block per step, five register chains

Each Montgomery ladder iteration is a single asm volatile block with RBP holding a pointer to a struct containing every field element at a literal offset. FE_ADD and FE_SUB are written to leave their five results in exactly the registers the following patch firing wants, so a chained consumer skips five reloads:

FE_ADD(A, x2, z2)        FE_SQ_FROM_REGS(AA)     // A never reloaded
FE_SUB(B, x2, z2)        FE_SQ_FROM_REGS(BB)
FE_SUB_NOSTORE(x3, z3)   FE_MUL_FROM_REGS_A(DA, A)   // D never stored either
FE_ADD_NOSTORE(x3, z3)   FE_MUL_FROM_REGS_A(CB, B)

Steps 6–9 are reordered 7-8-6-9: computing D before C lets both D→DA and C→CB chain, where the natural order chains only one. The dataflow is unchanged — C and D both depend only on x3 and z3, and feed different multiplies.

Dead stores

The step was issuing 90 stores per iteration and 25 of them were never read: C and D are consumed from registers and their slots read by nothing; t0 is written four times per step and three of those writes are dead. Removing them — 6,375 dead stores per scalar multiplication — was worth 3,179 cycles, measured against a control band of −776 to +395 across four untouched contenders at a fixed build configuration. The remaining 65 stores are all live.

Inversion

The Fermat chain's 254 squarings fall into runs of 1, 2, 1, 5, 10, 20, 10, 50, 100, 50, 5. Each run is one .rept inside the same asm block: the head loads, the tail stores, and every intermediate stays in registers with four register renames between firings. Only run boundaries touch memory.

Part II

What to put in front of a reviewer

Ordered by how well each claim survives someone actively trying to break it. The weakest thing you can do is lead with a ranking.

7 — The claims

Make these four, in this order

Claim 1

The arithmetic ties the fastest hand-tuned assembly for this field

With each side's invocation floor removed, microcode's field multiplication is 86.6 cycles against OpenSSL fe51's 87.0 — 0.4% apart. Both floors are measured, not modelled: 16.2 for microcode (real wrapper, one-triad no-op patch) and 10.1 for a native call (same call shape, same 10-load/5-store traffic, no arithmetic).

Say parity, never a win. A 0.4% margin on a derived quantity evaporates if a referee disputes either floor by two cycles, and it buys nothing. Quote fe_mul only — see the caution below.

Claim 2

1.26–1.35× over every other 5×51 backend, on all 24 configurations

On one fixed C ladder with only the field operations swapped, microcode beats fiat-crypto, CryptOpt, the amd64-51 assembly and hand-written C in 24 of 24 matched compiler and optimisation configurations. This is the strongest unqualified result, and it does not depend on the floor argument at all.

Claim 3

Benefit scales with work per firing — and that is the paper's finding

Entering patch RAM costs a flat 16.2 cycles regardless of operand count; an equivalent native call costs 10.1 for a multiply's ten loads and 6.4 for a square's five. Over 2,561 firings the differential is 20,285 cycles, and the measured same-ladder gap to OpenSSL is 21,087 — 96% accounted for, with the non-field residue matching to 2,833 cycles on an identical ladder.

This is what unifies both case studies. Keccak amortises one entry over 1,396 triads and wins by 1.084×; X25519 pays it 2,561 times and ties. Two ends of one measured curve, not two unrelated results.

Claim 4

The negative result is a boundary, not an embarrassment

The same replacement in a saturated 4×64 representation runs at 0.595× — microcode 1.68× slower than the assembly it replaces, ladder held constant. The unsaturated form leaves thirteen spare bits per limb so carries can be accumulated explicitly; the saturated form has no headroom and must propagate through RFLAGS, destroying the independent carry structure. Its 75-triad multiplier also leaves no room for a dedicated squarer under the 128-triad cap, so squaring becomes mul(a,a).

Lead with this rather than burying it. It converts "microcode is faster" into "microcode is faster when the representation exposes a carry structure the patch can exploit and the kernels fit" — a much harder claim to attack, and the one actually supported.

8 — The traps

Retire these before submission

Do not say

"Fastest 5×51" or "lowest kernel cost"

Both are false with OpenSSL in the table. It beats us at kernel level (97.1 / 73.2 against 102.8 / 81.8) and end to end (253,971 against 277,794), and it has been in the benchmark binary the whole time — which makes an omission harder to explain than the loss. s2n-bignum and the OpenSSL-on-our-ladder control belong in the tables too.

Handle with care

The fe_sq parity figure

Arithmetic-only fe_sq reads 0.982× in our favour, but the comparison flatters us: FE_SQ's lea/imul precompute sits inside the microcode floor and is subtracted away, while OpenSSL's fe51_sqr keeps its doubling inside the routine. fe_mul has no such asymmetry — its 19·bj precompute is inside the patch. Quote the multiply, or state the asymmetry in the same breath.

Do not ship

The five-accumulator fe_sq

It is correct in simulation (0/3000), 41 triads against 42, and 2.9× shallower — depth 20.5 against 59.3 — and would be worth about 17,000 cycles. It also hard-resets the machine. Five crashes. The most recent attempt eliminated the one µop form the stable fe_mul never uses (same-register MUL) and it still died. Do not cite predicted numbers from an unshippable patch.

10 — Questions to expect

Four a referee will ask

"Is the comparison fair — you time your own code inline and everyone else through a call."

Answered by measuring both floors. The kernel table reports full invocation cost for every backend; Table K5 strips a symmetric floor from each side. The harness is, if anything, tilted toward microcode — sixteen operations per asm block with no function boundary against a real call per operation for the natives — and microcode still loses to OpenSSL at that level.

"Is it constant time?"

No data-dependent branch and no data-dependent addressing. The patches are straight-line; every field element resolves through a fixed displacement; the conditional swap is mask-and-XOR with no branch. The same assumption every constant-time native implementation makes — that the underlying operations have data-independent latency — applies here too.

"Does this generalise beyond one core?"

No, and say so plainly. This is one microarchitecture, one patch RAM size, one hook mechanism. The 4×64 control shows it does not even generalise across representations on the same core. The contribution is that the microcode interface is a different interface with measurable consequences, not that it is universally faster.

"What about installation cost?"

Once at startup, outside every timed region. Worth one sentence rather than silence.

11 — Methodology

Disclose the measurement, including what nearly went wrong

  • Pinned. Turbo disabled, userspace governor at base. RDTSC ticks at the TSC's nominal 1.094 GHz while the core bursts to 2.4, so an unpinned run reports every number about 2.18× too fast with the ranking intact. That is the shape that reads as a breakthrough. Both the sweep and, now, the standalone binaries refuse to run unpinned.
  • Same process, interleaved. One repetition of every contender per round, round-robin, so drift lands equally on all of them rather than on whoever ran first. Verified flat: 1, 2, 4 and 15 contenders give the same result to 0.03%.
  • Best of 24 configurations per implementation — three gcc and three clang releases × four optimisation levels — matching how SUPERCOP selects. Three runs per configuration reduced to a median of medians.
  • Correctness gated in-process. All fifteen contenders pass RFC 7748 before any timing, in the same binary that then reports the numbers.
  • Frequency bracketed before and after the sweep; the artifact records the drift (+0.000% on this run).
Worth a footnote in the paper

Two measurement faults were found and fixed on the final day. A stale column list in the table generator silently discarded three contenders — including OpenSSL — that the sweep had been measuring all along, because zip() stops at the shorter sequence. And EXTRA_CPPFLAGS, documented in two source files as the way to select a parked patch, was referenced nowhere in the Makefile, so every such build silently produced the shipped one. Both now fail loudly. A reviewer who asks how you know your tables match your code deserves a better answer than "they should".

Hardware. Intel Celeron N3350, Goldmont, core 0, taskset -c 0, turbo off, userspace governor at 1094400 kHz; delivered core 1100 MHz against a 1094 MHz TSC, so ticks × 1.00548 = core cycles. That correction is common-mode and every ratio here is invariant to it.
