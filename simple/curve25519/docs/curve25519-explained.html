1. What Curve25519 is actually for

When two computers that have never met want to agree on a secret key over a public wire, they use a Diffie–Hellman key exchange. X25519 is the modern, fast, widely-deployed version of that handshake — it's inside TLS (so, inside almost every HTTPS connection), SSH, Signal, WireGuard, and more.

The whole thing rests on one operation that is easy to do and practically impossible to undo:

shared_secret = scalar · point

Here scalar is your 32-byte private key (just a big number), and point is a position on a specific curve called Curve25519. "Multiplying" a point by a number means adding the point to itself that many times, using the curve's special addition rule. Given the scalar and the point, computing the result is fast. Given only the result and the point, recovering the scalar is the "elliptic-curve discrete log problem" — believed to take longer than the age of the universe. That asymmetry is the whole game.

The punchline for this document: that "·" — the scalar multiplication — is 99% of the CPU time. And almost all of that is just multiplying and adding big numbers modulo a specific prime. So if you want a fast X25519, you need a fast big-number multiply. Everything below is about making that one multiply as fast as physically possible.

2. The only thing that runs hot: the ladder

To compute scalar · point, X25519 uses the Montgomery ladder. You don't need the geometry — just the shape of the work. The scalar is 255 bits. The ladder walks those bits one at a time, top to bottom, and at every single bit it does a fixed little routine called a ladder step:

So one X25519 ≈ 255 ladder steps ≈ roughly 1280 multiplications + 1270 squarings, plus a final "inversion" at the end (which is itself ~254 more squarings and 11 multiplications). Each of those mul/sq operations works on numbers modulo the prime 2255−19. Make that one operation faster and everything gets faster, proportionally.

3. The field: numbers modulo 2255−19

All the arithmetic happens in a finite field: the integers from 0 up to p−1, where

p = 2255 − 19

"Modulo p" just means: do normal integer math, then take the remainder after dividing by p. Every number stays a ~255-bit value. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and even division (multiply by an inverse) all work, and results always wrap back into the range 0 … p−1.

The prime 2255−19 was chosen on purpose to make the wrap-around cheap. Here is the one identity that the whole implementation leans on. Since p = 2255 − 19, we have 2255 ≡ 19 (mod p). In words:

The magic reduction: any time a bit lands at position 255 or higher, you can slide it 255 positions down and multiply it by 19. A "huge" overflow doesn't need a real division — it just becomes "× 19, added back into the bottom." This one fact is why Curve25519 is fast, and you'll see it appear everywhere below.

4. The representation: 5 limbs of 51 bits (and why)

A 255-bit number is too big for one 64-bit CPU register. The obvious choice is to use four 64-bit registers (4 × 64 = 256 bits). That's called the saturated representation, because every register is packed full.

We use something different and, for this job, smarter: five registers, each holding only 51 bits of the number. The top 13 bits of each 64-bit register are left empty. This is the unsaturated radix-251 representation:

value = a₀ + a₁·251 + a₂·2102 + a₃·2153 + a₄·2204

Why leave bits empty? Three reasons.

(1) Carries can be delayed. When you add two limbs, the result might be a little bigger than 51 bits — but it fits in the 13 bits of headroom without overflowing the 64-bit register. So you can add several things into a limb before you ever have to clean up. In the saturated 4×64 format, every single add can overflow, so you must handle the carry immediately, every time.

(2) The carries stay small and independent. Because each limb is only 51 bits, the amount that "spills" into the next limb is at most 13 bits. Small spills. And one limb's spill doesn't depend on a spill buried deep inside another. (Hold onto this — it's the reason the microcode trick works.)

(3) The ×19 reduction drops in for free. Limb 4 sits at weight 2204. A product of two limb-4 values lands at weight 2408. Watch:

2408 = 2153 · 2255 ≡ 2153 · 19 (mod p)

So a product that "falls off the top" comes right back as "× 19, into a lower limb." No division, no separate reduction pass — just a multiply by 19 folded into the sum. This is the magic reduction from §3, made concrete.

5. Wait — what gets split into limbs a and b?

This is the step that trips everyone up. The headline back in §1 was shared_secret = scalar · point, and the multiply (next section) works on two numbers a and b chopped into limbs. So it's natural to guess a = scalar and b = point. That is not what happens.

The scalar is the recipe, not an ingredient. Its 255 bits are read one at a time, and each bit only decides which way the ladder steps (a conditional swap of two working values). The scalar is never fed into a multiply. What the multiplies actually crunch are coordinates: the point you started from, plus all the running intermediates the ladder builds out of it.

A "point" is really just a number

On Curve25519 a point is pinned down by a single number — its x-coordinate, usually written u. That number lives in the field: an integer somewhere between 0 and p−1, i.e. a ~255-bit value. The 32-byte public key that arrives on the wire is that number, just written out as bytes. Everything the ladder then computes — the working values A, B, AA, BB, … from §2 — is another field element of exactly the same kind. So when a step runs fe_mul(a, b), a and b are simply two of these field elements, each already stored as 5 limbs.

Unpacking 32 bytes into 5 limbs (this is the actual "split")

The split into limbs happens once, when a 32-byte value is first read in. 32 bytes is 256 bits; we take the bottom 255 and cut them into five 51-bit slices. The wrinkle: 51 is not a multiple of 8, so the slices don't land on byte boundaries — four bytes end up straddling two limbs:

Here is the real unpacking (fe_frombytes), shortened. Watch byte 6: its low 3 bits (& 0x07) finish off limb 0, and its remaining bits (>> 3) start limb 1:

/* fe_frombytes: 32 input bytes  ->  5 limbs of 51 bits */
t[0] =  in[0]      | in[1]<<8  | in[2]<<16 | in[3]<<24
      | in[4]<<32 | in[5]<<40 | (in[6]  & 0x07) << 48;   // limb a0 : bits 0–50
t[1] = (in[6]>>3)  | in[7]<<5  | ...        | (in[12] & 0x3f) << 45;   // limb a1 : bits 51–101
t[2] = (in[12]>>6) | in[13]<<2 | ...        | (in[19] & 0x01) << 50;   // limb a2 : bits 102–152
t[3] = (in[19]>>1) | in[20]<<7 | ...        | (in[25] & 0x0f) << 47;   // limb a3 : bits 153–203
t[4] = (in[25]>>4) | in[26]<<4 | ...        | (in[31] & 0x7f) << 44;   // limb a4 : bits 204–254

After this, a (and likewise b) is five 64-bit registers, 51 bits used in each. That is the form the multiply grid in the next section expects. The reverse — packing 5 limbs back into 32 bytes for the wire — happens just once at the very end (fe_tobytes).

6. Multiplication: the 5×5 grid and the ×19 fold

To multiply two field elements a and b, you multiply every limb of a by every limb of b — 5 × 5 = 25 small products. Product aᵢ · bⱼ has weight 251(i+j), so it belongs to output limb i+j. When i+j ≥ 5 it falls off the top and comes back × 19 into limb i+j−5:

Collecting the cells gives the five output limbs. This is exactly the C "reference" code we test against — it reads just like the grid:

/* from full_curve25519_inline2.c — the plain-C multiply we verify against */
r1=19*b1; r2=19*b2; r3=19*b3; r4=19*b4;   // pre-scale the wrap-around limbs

c0 = a0*b0 + a1*r4 + a2*r3 + a3*r2 + a4*r1;   // the ×19 cells folded in
c1 = a0*b1 + a1*b0 + a2*r4 + a3*r3 + a4*r2;
c2 = a0*b2 + a1*b1 + a2*b0 + a3*r4 + a4*r3;
c3 = a0*b3 + a1*b2 + a2*b1 + a3*b0 + a4*r4;
c4 = a0*b4 + a1*b3 + a2*b2 + a3*b1 + a4*b0;

Each cᵢ is a sum of five 64×64→128-bit products, so it needs a 128-bit accumulator. Five sums, twenty-five products. That's the multiply.

7. Squaring: the same grid, folded in half

Squaring is a · a, so the grid is symmetric: aᵢ·aⱼ is the same product as aⱼ·aᵢ. Instead of computing both, you compute it once and double it. That cuts 25 products down to 15. The doublings (like 2·a₁) and the ×19 folds (like 19·a₃) are computed once, up front — in the real implementation the caller prepares them with cheap lea and imul instructions before handing the limbs to the kernel:

/* from FE_SQ — operands are pre-shaped before the field op runs */
lea  r15, [rdi + rdi]   ; r15 = 2·a0
lea  r13, [rsi + rsi]   ; r13 = 2·a1
lea  r9,  [r12 + r12]   ; r9  = 2·a2
lea  r10, [r11 + r11]   ; r10 = 2·a3
imul rbx, r14, 19        ; rbx = 19·a4
imul rdx, r11, 19        ; rdx = 19·a3

The squaring formula then has only 15 products spread across the five limbs (this is the C we verify against):

/* d_i = 2·a_i (doublings), r3 = 19·a3, r4 = 19·a4 */
c0 = a0*a0 + d1*r4 + d2*r3;
c1 = d0*a1 + r3*a3 + d2*r4;
c2 = d0*a2 + a1*a1 + d3*r4;
c3 = d0*a3 + d1*a2 + r4*a4;
c4 = d0*a4 + d1*a3 + a2*a2;
Squaring is cheaper than multiplication (15 products vs 25), and a ladder does more squarings than multiplications. So squaring is the operation worth optimizing hardest — and, as you'll see in §14, it's where we win the most.

8. Carrying: getting back to neat 51-bit limbs

After the grid, each cᵢ is a fat number (up to ~107 bits in the 128-bit accumulator). We need to squeeze it back into five tidy 51-bit limbs. The overflow above bit 51 is the carry — it gets added into the next limb up:

/* the carry chain, plain C */
carry = c0 >> 51;  out0 = c0 & MASK51;   c1 += carry;
carry = c1 >> 51;  out1 = c1 & MASK51;   c2 += carry;
carry = c2 >> 51;  out2 = c2 & MASK51;   c3 += carry;
carry = c3 >> 51;  out3 = c3 & MASK51;   c4 += carry;
carry = c4 >> 51;  out4 = c4 & MASK51;
out0 += carry * 19;   // top carry wraps: ×19 back into limb 0  (magic reduction again!)
carry = out0 >> 51; out0 &= MASK51; out1 += carry;

Notice the last two lines: the carry out of the top limb doesn't have a "next limb" — it has fallen off the top at weight 2255, so it comes back as × 19 into limb 0. The magic reduction, one more time.

That & MASK51 / >> 51 dance is the carry propagation. It is dead simple in C. The next two sections are about why it is not simple for the CPU, and how microcode beats it.

9. Why a normal CPU struggles with this

Look at the carry chain again. c1 += carry can't start until carry = c0 >> 51 is done. c2 += carry waits on c1. It's a chain — each step depends on the one before. Chains can't be parallelized.

Worse, on x86 the natural way to add multi-word numbers is the add-with-carry instruction (ADC), and there is exactly one carry flag in the whole CPU. Every ADC reads it and writes it. So a long carry chain is forced to run one instruction at a time, single-file, through that one shared flag.

The target chip (Intel Goldmont) is a small in-order, 2-ALU core. It can do two simple operations per cycle — but only if they're independent. The multiplications in our grid are independent and fast. The carry chain is a single-file dependent line. So the chip ends up waiting on carries while the multiplier sits idle.

The bottleneck in one line: the multiplies are not the problem — the carries are, because they're forced to go single-file through one carry flag.

10. The microcode idea: triads, hooks, one "firing"

x86 instructions don't run directly on the silicon. Each one is decoded into tiny internal steps called micro-ops, and on this chip those come from a patchable microcode RAM. With the right "red unlock" you can write your own micro-ops into that RAM. That's the layer we're working at — below the instruction set.

The micro-ops are grouped into triads: a bundle of up to 3 micro-ops that issue together, plus a "sequence word" controlling flow. Our entire field operation is just a list of triads (66 for multiply, 42 for square).

How we get our code to run: a hook

We pick an x86 instruction that's harmless in our context — vmwrite — and hook it: we tell the microcode "when you see vmwrite, jump into our patch instead." Now the native code triggers a whole field multiply by issuing a single vmwrite. We call that one event a firing.

One firing = one whole field operation. No loop inside, no bouncing back and forth between normal code and microcode mid-operation. The native side fires once; the patch computes all 25 products, all the carries, the reduction; control returns with the 5 result limbs sitting in registers.

Two operations resident at once

A hook maps one instruction to one patch. We need both multiply and square. So we use two hooks on two different harmless instructions:

operationtriadslives atfired by
multiply66U7c00vmwrite (0x0cd8)
square42U7d08vmread (0x0618)

That's 66 + 42 = 108 triads, comfortably under the 128-triad limit of the patch RAM. Having two separate trigger instructions is what lets a single block of assembly freely mix squares (vmread) and multiplies (vmwrite) without them stepping on each other — which matters a lot in §13.

11. Core trick #1: three carry chains at once

Here's the first real win. Inside one triad we can issue up to 3 micro-ops, and crucially they can use per-register carry flags (via an op called SETCC) — not just the one shared CPU flag. So instead of one single-file carry chain, we run three independent running totals at the same time:

And because a triad bundles 3 micro-ops, we fuse the work: one triad can do a multiply, an add into an accumulator, and capture a carry — all together — while the next product is already starting. Here are two real triads from the squaring patch (op names simplified for readability):

// TMP0 = low accumulator, R8 = high accumulator,
// TMP9 = carry-sum, TMP15 = the just-captured carry bit, RCX = a product's high half
{ ADD(TMP0,TMP0,RDI),  SETCC(TMP15,TMP0), ADD(R8,R8,RCX)      }  // add a low half ; grab its carry ; add a high half
{ ZEROEXT(TMP9,TMP15), MUL(RCX,RBX,R13),  ADD(TMP0,TMP0,R13) }  // stash the carry ; start NEXT product ; add its low half

The first triad does three dependent-but-bundled things in one shot: add a low half, snapshot the carry it produced, and fold in a high half. The second triad launches the next multiply while still accumulating — so a multiply, an add, and a carry capture all overlap. A normal CPU would spend separate instructions and serialize the carry through its one flag. This fusion + the three parallel chains is the whole structural advantage.

12. Core trick #2: 264 = 251 · 213

At the end of a limb we have to turn those three accumulators back into one limb value plus a carry into the next limb. First we fold the carry-sum into the high accumulator, so the limb's true value is:

S = TMP0 + 264 · R8

We need two things from S: the bottom 51 bits (the output limb), and everything above bit 51 (the carry into the next limb). The clever part is the carry. Watch what 264 does when you divide by 251:

carry = ⌊S / 251⌋ = (TMP0 >> 51) + 213·R8 = (TMP0 >> 51) | (R8 << 13)

Why does + become | (a bitwise OR)? Because the two pieces never touch the same bits:

And the output limb? It's just S mod 251, which (since the R8 part is a multiple of 251) equals TMP0 mod 251 — done with a shift-left-13 then shift-right-13 (clears the top, no mask constant needed). Here is the real closing sequence and the one-OR link that opens the next limb:

{ ADD(R8,R8,TMP9),  SHL(TMP6,TMP0,13),  SHL(TMP1,R8,13)  }  // fold carry-sum ; mask-prep ; high carry = R8<<13
{ SHR(out0,TMP6,13), SHR(TMP8,TMP0,51), ...            }  // out0 = TMP0 mod 2⁵¹ ; low carry = TMP0>>51
// ... next limb begins:
{ OR(TMP0,TMP8,TMP1), MUL(...), ADD(TMP0,...) }  // LINK: TMP0 = lowcarry | highcarry, then start accumulating again

The very last carry, off the top of limb 4, wraps with the magic reduction: one multiply by 19 back into limb 0, one short propagation, done.

13. Bolting it into X25519: register chaining

Now we have two fast field ops, each fired by one instruction. The naive way to build X25519 is to call them like functions, bouncing every intermediate result out to memory and back. We do better: the whole scalar multiplication is written as inline assembly in one block, with all 15 working values living in a fixed stack structure, and consecutive operations chained through registers.

Register chaining: we arranged the add/subtract helpers to leave their results in exactly the registers the next multiply or square wants as input. So "add, then square the sum" hands off register-to-register — no store, no reload, no memory bounce for the intermediate.

For example, the add helper leaves its 5 result limbs in {rdi, rsi, r12, r11, r14} — which is precisely the input register set for the square. So this pair chains for free:

// one piece of a ladder step: A = x2 + z2, then AA = A²
FE_ADD(A_OFF, X2_OFF, Z2_OFF)      // result lands in {rdi,rsi,r12,r11,r14}
FE_SQ_FROM_REGS(AA_OFF)            // reads those same regs directly — no reload

A single ladder step has six such register chains. We even reorder operations so independent chains line up and run back-to-back. The Montgomery step dataflow looks like this:

The inversion: 254 squarings, almost all chained

At the very end, X25519 needs one division, done as zp−2 via Fermat's little theorem. That's an addition chain: 254 squarings broken into runs of 1, 2, 1, 5, 10, 20, 10, 50, 100, 50, 5, with 11 multiplies splicing the runs together. Inside a run, each square's output is renamed straight into the next square's input (4 register moves — the lowest limb already lines up), so a run of 100 squarings touches memory only at its two ends. That single block is hundreds of firings with almost no memory traffic in between.

The win: our microcode field ops make X25519 faster than every other 5×51 implementation — beating optimized C, the formally-verified fiat-crypto, the CryptOpt superoptimizer, donna, and even Bernstein–Schwabe's hand-written 5×51 assembly. The field-op advantage even survives being transplanted into someone else's framework (amd64-51/ucode beats amd64-51/asm).
The honest catch: the fastest X25519 overall is still amd64-64 hand assembly (271k vs our 300k, ~11% ahead). It uses a different number format — the saturated 4×64 — that happens to suit a normal CPU better. And when we port our microcode to that 4×64 format, it collapses to 511k (nearly 2× slower than the asm). Why? The 4×64 format needs long, full-width carry chains — and our microcode can't thread the CPU carry flag through a dependent chain inside a patch, so it has to fake it with slow serial steps. The exact thing the 5×51 format let us avoid (§9) comes roaring back. The format that's best for normal CPUs is the one our trick is worst at — and vice versa.

That's the whole story: a key exchange → a scalar multiply → a ladder of field ops → big-number multiply mod 2255−19 → a 5-limb format whose spare bits make carries small and the ×19 reduction free → microcode that runs three carry chains at once and links limbs with a single OR → a register-chained ladder that never bounces intermediates through memory. Fast where the format cooperates, honestly bounded where it doesn't.
