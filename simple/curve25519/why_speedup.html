Why the microcode X25519 is faster

Cycle-level attribution on the Intel Goldmont Celeron N3350 · 5×51 unsaturated GF(2²⁵⁵−19) · median cycles, taskset -c 0

The win is the microcode field operation, full stop. Replacing the compiled-C field mul/sq with a microcode patch makes each operation ~1.1–1.3× cheaper. Because an X25519 is ~2,560 dependent field operations and ~89% of its cycles live in the ladder, that per-op edge carries straight through to a 1.15× end-to-end win over the fiat-crypto reference (357,929 → 312,399 cyc on the identical ladder). The inline-asm register-chaining adds only a further ~2%. The microcode also matches hand-tuned CryptOpt asm — without anyone writing asm.

microcode mul = 123.0 cyc fiat mul = 141.3 cyc (1.15×) ours/ucode X25519 = 304,467 cyc RFC 7748: 23/23 ✓

1. The cost model: X25519 is firing-bound

The Montgomery ladder issues its field operations serially — each mul/sq feeds the next, so what matters is latency, not throughput. Counting the operations actually executed:

X25519 ≈ 255 ladder steps × 9 firings  +  invert (254 sq + 11 mul)  +  1 final mul
       = 2,295 + 265 + 1  ≈  2,561 microcode firings
cost ≈ 2,561 × ~120 cyc/firing  ≈  ~307k cyc  (measured: 304,467) ✓

Top-down, the measured budget of one X25519 splits as:

componentcycshare
ladder (255 × 1,062.5)270,93889%
fe_invert21,5337%
cswap (×512)~5,6001.8%
pack / unpack / final mul~6,0002%

Because ~96% of the cycles are field operations (ladder + invert), making one mul/sq cheaper is the only lever that moves the end-to-end number. Everything else (adds 10 cyc, cswap 11 cyc) is in the noise.

Why is the microcode op faster?

A 5×51 field multiply is 25 64-bit integer multiplies plus a serial carry/reduction chain mod 2²⁵⁵−19 — about 50–70 machine instructions when compiled. The Goldmont N3350 is a narrow, in-order Atom-class core: it decodes ~2 instructions/cycle and cannot reorder around the serial carry chain, so fiat/native bottleneck on the front-end and the dependency chain at ~140–166 cyc.

The microcode patch encodes the same arithmetic as triads (3 micro-ops + a sequencing word each) that execute directly in patch RAM, driving the hardware multiplier through a private register file and bypassing the fetch/decode/dispatch front-end entirely. The catch is a fixed “firing tax” — the cost of the hooked vmwrite/vmread trigger plus patch entry/exit.

Evidence that the tax — not the work — dominates: the mul patch is 66 triads and the sq patch is 42 triads, yet both measure ~123 cyc. The per-op cost is essentially independent of how much arithmetic the patch does, which means a fixed per-firing overhead sets the floor and the actual multiply/reduce is largely hidden behind it. Microcode reaches that floor (~123 cyc); compiled C cannot, because it pays the front-end tax on every one of its ~60 instructions.

This also explains the two boundaries of the result: microcode beats portable C clearly (it removes the front-end bottleneck) but only ties hand-tuned CryptOpt asm (sq is a dead heat) — both have reached the practical floor for this operation on this core; the microcode just gets there without hand-written asm.

3. The clean proof: same ladder, swap only the field op

The per-op numbers predict the end-to-end win, but to remove every confound we ran four X25519s on the identical C Montgomery ladder (same driver, invert, cswap, pack) and changed only fe_mul/fe_sq:

field-op backend (same C ladder)median cycratio vs microcode
microcode (ucode/C-ladder)312,3991.00×
fiat-crypto357,9291.15× slower
CryptOpt asm385,4971.23× slower
native (hand C)389,1371.25× slower

On a byte-for-byte identical ladder, the microcode field op alone takes X25519 from fiat's 357,929 to 312,399 cyc — a 1.15× win with zero other variables. That 1.15× matches the per-op mul ratio exactly, closing the loop: the field-op latency is the end-to-end win.

(CryptOpt loses more end-to-end than its near-tied per-op cost suggests: in the full ladder it is an external asm call per operation — call/return overhead that doesn't inline — whereas microcode and fiat are emitted inline.)

4. The inline-asm coding tricks add only ~2%

Our headline ours/ucode (304,467 cyc) also uses two register-chaining tricks on top of the microcode ops. Toggling each off shows they are a minor polish, not the source of the win:

trick disabledchainedplain (off)cost of the trick
ladder register-chaining1,062.5/step1,093.2/step~7.8k / X25519 (~2.5%)

The tricks only remove movs between firings — but a mov is ~1 cyc and a firing is ~123 cyc, so eliminating reloads buys almost nothing. The ladder coding is a ~2% effect; the microcode field op is the ~15% effect.

Honest framing. The microcode beats every 5×51 backend and matches tuned asm, but it does not beat amd64-64/asm (272,768 cyc). That implementation uses a different representation — 4×64 saturated (radix 2⁶⁴) — which needs fewer, wider limbs and is what lib25519's autotuner selects on Goldmont. The fair, like-for-like claim is: among 5×51 implementations, the microcode field op is the fastest, beating the fiat-crypto reference by 1.15× and matching hand-tuned asm.

6. The attribution, in one chain

  1. Per-op: the microcode mul/sq is ~1.1–1.3× cheaper than compiled C (123 cyc vs fiat 141 / native 159), because it bypasses the narrow Goldmont front-end and pays only a fixed firing tax.
  2. Structure: X25519 is firing-bound — ~2,561 dependent field ops, ~89% of cycles in the ladder — so a per-op win translates almost 1:1 to the end-to-end number.
  3. End-to-end (zero confound): on the identical C ladder, swapping in microcode field ops gives 312,399 vs fiat's 357,929 = 1.15×, matching the per-op ratio.
  4. Everything else is small: the inline-asm register-chaining adds ~2%; cswap, pack, and adds are negligible.
