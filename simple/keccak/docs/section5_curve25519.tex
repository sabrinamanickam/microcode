\section{Curve25519 in Microcode}
\label{sec:curve25519}

The second worked example carries the methodology of \Cref{sec:ucode-layer} from
a permutation onto field arithmetic. The Keccak study of \Cref{sec:keccak}
concerned a single large kernel, evaluated once per message block, whose
difficulty was that its state did not fit in the architectural register file. A
Curve25519 field operation presents the opposite profile in every dimension that
the microcode layer is sensitive to. It is small, it is straight-line, it is
dominated by integer multiplication, its operands occupy only a handful of
registers, and a single scalar multiplication invokes it on the order of a
thousand times. The object we place in the patch RAM is therefore not the scalar
multiplication but the two field operations beneath it, the multiplication and
the squaring modulo the prime $p = 2^{255}-19$, and the experimental question is
whether a hand-scheduled microcode encoding of those two operations can be made
faster than the best native code that exists for them: a modern optimising
compiler, a formally verified arithmetic generator, a dedicated cryptographic
superoptimiser, and the hand-written assembly of the primitive's own designers.

Because the field element fits comfortably in registers, the lever that drove
the Keccak result --- whole-state residency --- is unavailable and unnecessary
here. The lever instead is the structure of carry propagation, and the remainder
of this section is organised around it: \Cref{sec:c25519-field} establishes why
the carry chain, rather than the multiplication, is what binds a native
implementation; \Cref{sec:c25519-kernel} describes the triad kernel that breaks
that bind by maintaining three carry chains at once; \Cref{sec:c25519-coexist}
and \Cref{sec:c25519-budget} treat the installation of the two patches together
and the packing pressure peculiar to this primitive; \Cref{sec:c25519-assemble}
describes how the inlined scalar multiplication is built around the two patches;
and \Cref{sec:c25519-perf} reports the measurements and states plainly where the
advantage ends.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Representation and Layout}
\label{sec:c25519-field}

A field element is represented in the unsaturated radix-$2^{51}$ form: five
64-bit words, each holding 51 significant bits and leaving the upper 13 bits
free. This is the representation that fiat-crypto emits and that the
Bernstein--Schwabe \texttt{amd64-51} code uses, and adopting it unchanged is what
later permits the microcode field operations to be dropped into those native
frameworks without disturbing anything around them. A multiplication is the
$5\times5$ schoolbook of twenty-five partial products, and the reduction implied
by $2^{255}\equiv 19 \pmod p$ is folded into the cross terms so that the wrapped
products simply enter the lower output limbs scaled by $19$, with no separate
reduction pass:
\[
c_i \;=\; \sum_{j\le i} a_{\,i-j}\,b_j \;+\; 19\!\!\sum_{j>i} a_{\,5+i-j}\,b_j ,
\qquad 0 \le i < 5 .
\]
Squaring is encoded as a separate patch rather than reusing the multiplication,
because the symmetry $a_i a_j = a_j a_i$ collapses the twenty-five products to
fifteen, with the off-diagonal terms taken with a coefficient of two. The
coefficients this introduces --- the doublings $2a_i$ and the reduction folds
$19a_i$ --- are not recomputed inside the patch. They are produced once by the
caller using address-generation (\texttt{lea}) and integer-multiply
(\texttt{imul}) instructions on the native side and handed to the patch in
registers, so the kernel receives operands already shaped for its partial
products and spends none of its triads forming them.

\paragraph{Register layout.}
A field operation is carried out entirely in registers. The caller loads the five
limbs of each operand into a fixed set of general-purpose registers immediately
before the triggering instruction, and reads the five result limbs from a fixed set
of registers afterwards, so a field element is never spilled to memory while the
operation runs. The two operations differ only in what the caller must prepare in
advance. For squaring, the four doublings $2a_0,\dots,2a_3$ and the two folds
$19a_3$ and $19a_4$ are formed on the native side with address-generation and
integer-multiply instructions and delivered alongside the five limbs, and the result
returns in $\texttt{RDI},\texttt{R9},\texttt{R10},\texttt{RBX},\texttt{RAX}$. For
multiplication the caller delivers the second operand unmodified, and a three-triad
preparation stage at the head of the patch forms the folds $19b_1,\dots,19b_4$ in
place while parking the original limbs in temporary registers for the cross terms
that still require them, the result returning in
$\texttt{R15},\texttt{R13},\texttt{R9},\texttt{R10},\texttt{RAX}$. Throughout the
kernel a small, fixed set of temporary registers holds the running state of the
accumulation: one register accumulates the low halves of the partial products
(\texttt{TMP0}), a second accumulates their high halves (\texttt{R8}), a third sums
the carries that the low-half additions produce (\texttt{TMP9}), and two further
registers hold the low and high pieces of the carry passed from one limb to the next
(\texttt{TMP8} and \texttt{TMP1}). The kernel of \Cref{sec:c25519-kernel} is written
against exactly this layout, and it is this register-resident discipline that later
permits successive field operations to be chained to one another without a return to
memory, as \Cref{sec:c25519-assemble} describes.

A consequence of leaving thirteen bits free in every limb is that the carries
produced while the partial products are summed are short and mutually independent.
Because each limb is reduced to 51 bits, the value carried into the next limb
occupies at most the complementary 13 bits, and the carry out of one limb does not
depend on a carry buried inside another. This is a property of the layout itself,
independent of any particular implementation, and the kernel of
\Cref{sec:c25519-kernel} is built around exploiting it.

\paragraph{Where the advantage comes from, and where it stops.}
It is worth stating precisely what this representation buys the microcode, because
the point is easy to overstate. Within the $5\times51$ representation the binding
resource for native code is carry propagation, not multiplication. The x86-64
architecture exposes a single carry flag, so a native accumulation must serialise
its carries through the add-with-carry instruction, each instance of which both
reads and writes that one flag, and the two-arithmetic-unit in-order target core
cannot overlap that dependent flag chain with the independent multiplications that
feed it. The microcode layer's advantage lies exactly here: it advances several
carry chains at once, each on its own private flag, in the manner that
\Cref{sec:c25519-kernel} describes, and this is what makes it the fastest
$5\times51$ field backend we measured (\Cref{sec:c25519-perf}). It is not, however,
a claim that carry propagation is the bottleneck of X25519 in general, and a single
observation makes the limit plain: the fastest implementation on this core,
\texttt{amd64-64}, is itself built on long add-with-carry chains and still wins.
It does so for two reasons that have nothing to do with carry handling. First, its
saturated $4\times64$ algorithm performs fewer multiplications --- sixteen partial
products against our twenty-five --- on a core whose multiplier, not its carry
logic, is the scarce resource. Second, being ordinary native code it pays none of
the per-firing dispatch overhead that our hooked operations incur on the order of
two and a half thousand times per scalar multiplication. The honest measure of the
layer's advantage is therefore the comparison in which only the field operations
change: with the microcode substituted into the \texttt{amd64-51} framework, leaving
the ladder, inversion, and swap untouched, it is about eight percent faster end to
end, and the squaring and multiplication on their own are roughly twenty-six and
three percent faster than fiat-crypto. The microcode is, in short, the fastest
$5\times51$ X25519 we measured; it is not the fastest X25519, remaining about eleven
percent behind \texttt{amd64-64}, whose advantage is one of algorithm and native
execution rather than of carries.

\subsection{The Kernel: One Firing, Three Carry Chains}
\label{sec:c25519-kernel}

An entire field multiplication, or an entire field squaring, is performed by a
single execution of one hooked macro-instruction --- one firing. The native side
issues that instruction; the patch then computes every partial product, every
inter-limb carry, and the final modular reduction within the register file, and
returns with the five result limbs in place. Nothing about the operation loops
inside the patch, and control never crosses back and forth between native code
and microcode in the middle of an operation. The kernel is consequently a single
flat schedule of triads, and its entire art lies in how densely the
multiplications, accumulations, and carries are packed into the three operation
slots of each triad.

\paragraph{Three accumulators that never share the carry flag.}
The kernel forms each output limb as a sum of products held in three independent
running totals, none of which uses the architectural carry flag. The low halves
of the partial products accumulate into a register we will call the low
accumulator (\texttt{TMP0}); the high halves accumulate into a high accumulator
(\texttt{R8}); and the carries thrown off by the low-half additions are summed
into a third register (\texttt{TMP9}). The double-width product itself is split
at the multiply: the instruction is encoded so that its low half is written into
the operand register, where it is read and added into the low accumulator in the
very next slot, while its high half is written into a scratch register
(\texttt{RCX}) to be added into the high accumulator. The carry produced by each
low-half addition is captured by a \texttt{SETCC} into a per-register flag domain
of the kind established in \Cref{sec:findings}, which is the property that makes
the whole scheme possible: because each chain captures its carry into its own
private flag, the carry capture fits in a spare slot of the same triad that
issues the next multiplication, rather than serialising on one shared flag behind
it. \Cref{lst:c25519-mac} shows the resulting motif, in which a multiplication,
the accumulation of a partial product, and a carry capture co-reside in two
triads while the next product is already in flight.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={The fused multiply--accumulate--carry
motif, taken from the squaring kernel's first output limb. \texttt{TMP0} is the
low accumulator, \texttt{R8} the high accumulator, \texttt{TMP9} the running sum
of accumulation carries, \texttt{TMP15} the transient \texttt{SETCC} carry. The
multiply writes its low half into the operand register (read on the same line)
and its high half into \texttt{RCX} (added in
later).},label={lst:c25519-mac},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
{ ZEROEXT(TMP0,RAX),   MUL(RCX,RDI,RDI),    NOP,               NOP_SEQW } // TMP0:=0 ; product a0*a0 -> (hi=RCX, lo=RDI)
{ ADD(TMP0,TMP0,RDI),  SETCC(TMP15,TMP0),   ADD(R8,R8,RCX),    NOP_SEQW } // acc low half ; capture carry ; acc previous high half
{ ZEROEXT(TMP9,TMP15), MUL(RCX,RBX,R13),    ADD(TMP0,TMP0,R13),NOP_SEQW } // seed carry sum ; next product ; acc its low half
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Resolving a limb and linking to the next.}
At the end of a limb the three accumulators are reconciled into one canonical
limb value and one carry into the following limb, by the short sequence of
\Cref{lst:c25519-carry}. The carry sum is first folded into the high accumulator,
so that \texttt{R8} then represents the entire contribution of weight $2^{64}$ and
above and \texttt{TMP0} the contribution below it; the full accumulated value of
the limb is $S = \texttt{TMP0} + 2^{64}\,\texttt{R8}$. The output limb is the
residue $S \bmod 2^{51}$, and the carry into the next limb is the quotient
$\lfloor S/2^{51}\rfloor$. The kernel exploits the identity
$2^{64} = 2^{51}\cdot 2^{13}$ to compute both cheaply. The quotient separates into
a low contribution $\texttt{TMP0} \gg 51$, which is at most 13 bits wide, and a
high contribution $\texttt{R8} \ll 13$, whose low 13 bits are necessarily zero;
the two are therefore disjoint in their bit positions, and their sum is realised
by a single bitwise \texttt{OR}. That \texttt{OR} is the whole of the inter-limb
link: it seeds the low accumulator of the next limb. The output limb itself,
$\texttt{TMP0} \bmod 2^{51}$, is produced by shifting left by 13 and then right by
13, which clears the upper bits with no mask constant and no spare register. After
the last limb the remaining top carry is folded back into limb zero by a single
multiplication by the constant $19$, one short propagation from limb zero into
limb one completes the reduction modulo $p$, and the result is left lazily reduced
to at most 52 bits in one limb, which the representation tolerates.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={Limb resolution and the inter-limb link.
Because $2^{64}=2^{51}\cdot2^{13}$, the carry out of a limb splits into a 13-bit
low part (\texttt{TMP8}) and a left-shifted high part (\texttt{TMP1}) whose bit
ranges do not overlap, so the link that opens the next limb is an \texttt{OR},
not an addition.},label={lst:c25519-carry},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
{ ADD(R8,R8,TMP9),    SHL(TMP6,TMP0,13),   SHL(TMP1,R8,13),   NOP_SEQW } // fold carry sum ; mask-prep ; high carry = R8<<13
{ SHR(out0,TMP6,13),  SHR(TMP8,TMP0,51),   NOTAND(R8,R8,R8),  NOP_SEQW } // out0 = TMP0 mod 2^51 ; low carry ; clear R8
// ... the next limb opens with the link and its first product ...
{ OR(TMP0,TMP8,TMP1), MUL(RCX,R15,R13),    ADD(TMP0,TMP0,R13),NOP_SEQW } // TMP0 := lowcarry | highcarry ; next product
\end{lstlisting}

This is the field-arithmetic realisation of the structural claim the paper makes
for the layer as a whole. The native multiplication is bound by a single carry
chain threaded through one flag; the microcode runs three accumulation chains in
parallel on private flags and folds each carry capture into a multiplication's own
triad. The work the layer performs --- concurrent carry propagation --- is exactly
the work the instruction set cannot express, just as whole-state residency was for
Keccak.

\paragraph{Authoring and verification.}
Unlike the generator-emitted Keccak round, these two kernels are scheduled by
hand. At a few tens of triads each they are small enough to lay out manually, and
their dense, irregular interleaving of multiplies and carries gives a generic
packer little to improve; they are nonetheless held to the slot and hazard rules
of \Cref{sec:triads-packing}. Each patch is checked against the fiat-crypto
reference --- the same backend the superoptimiser targets --- over a large random
corpus and a thousand-iteration self-chaining test, and the assembled scalar
multiplication is checked against the RFC~7748 known-answer vectors before any
timing is reported.

\subsection{Installing Both Operations at Once}
\label{sec:c25519-coexist}

The multiplication and squaring patches reside in the patch RAM at the same time:
the multiplication occupies 66 triads beginning at \texttt{U7c00}, and the squaring
occupies 42 triads beginning immediately after it at \texttt{U7d08}. Since one
hooked macro-instruction can be redirected to only a single patch entry point,
holding two operations resident requires two match registers intercepting two
different instructions, in the manner of \Cref{sec:triads-packing}. We hook the
multiplication to \texttt{vmwrite} (opcode \texttt{0x0cd8}) and the squaring to
\texttt{vmread} (opcode \texttt{0x0618}); the native side fires a multiplication by
issuing a \texttt{vmwrite} and a squaring by issuing a \texttt{vmread}, the latter
emitted as raw opcode bytes since the operation is otherwise inert in the
unprivileged context. The combined $66+42 = 108$ triads sit within the 128-triad
capacity with room to spare. This dual installation is not a convenience; it is
what allows a single block of inline assembly to interleave squarings and
multiplications freely, which is the foundation of the integration described in
\Cref{sec:c25519-assemble}, since each kind of operation has its own dedicated
entry instruction and the two never collide.

\subsection{Triad Budget: Density, Not Capacity}
\label{sec:c25519-budget}

For Keccak the binding constraint of \Cref{sec:budget} was capacity: a single
round body strained against the 128-triad ceiling. For Curve25519 the constraint
is inverted. At 108 triads the two operations leave the budget unexhausted, and
the design pressure is not whether the kernel fits but how tightly it packs,
because every multiply--accumulate--carry sequence folded into a single triad
shortens both the static patch and its dynamic critical path. The development of
these kernels is largely a history of such merges. The multiplication began near
88 triads and was brought to 73 and then to 66 by, among other steps, packing the
three preparatory reduction folds into one triad and pulling each product's carry
capture out of a dedicated triad and into the free third slot of a
multiply--accumulate triad. The squaring's last output limb was compressed from
eight triads to seven by exploiting a cross-slot dependence within a single triad.
Each such merge is legitimate only because the intra-triad hazard behaviour
recorded in \Cref{sec:findings} guarantees that the three slots of a triad observe
one another's reads and writes in program order; the packing rests directly on
that empirical result.

\subsection{Assembling X25519 Around the Patches}
\label{sec:c25519-assemble}

A scalar multiplication invokes the field operations on the order of a thousand
times: a 255-step Montgomery ladder with roughly ten field operations per step,
followed by a Fermat inversion $z^{p-2}$ that is itself a long chain of squarings.
The fastest implementation we built, which we call the inlined ladder, expresses
this entire computation as inline assembly built directly on the two patches,
rather than as portable C that reaches them through function wrappers. The fifteen
field elements a ladder step manipulates live together in one stack-resident
state structure at fixed byte offsets, addressed throughout the step through a
base register pinned to that structure, and the firings are emitted within one
large assembly block per step. The compiler is thus obliged to allocate and spill
only around the block as a whole, never between individual field operations.

\paragraph{Chaining through registers.}
The decisive integration technique is to choose the caller conventions so that one
operation's outputs land in exactly the registers the next operation reads as
inputs, eliminating the store and reload that would otherwise separate them. The
addition and subtraction macros are written to deposit their five result limbs in
the same register set that the squaring and multiplication patches consume as
their first operand. An addition that feeds a squaring, or a subtraction that feeds
a multiplication, therefore becomes a single chain with no intervening memory
traffic for the intermediate. The principal block of a ladder step contains six
such chains; the simplest is the addition $A=x_2+z_2$ feeding the squaring
$AA=A^2$, and the others are $B=x_2-z_2$ into $B^2$, $D=x_3-z_3$ into $D\!\cdot\!A$,
$C=x_3+z_3$ into $C\!\cdot\!B$, and the sum and difference of $D\!\cdot\!A$ and
$C\!\cdot\!B$ each into a squaring. Two of these chains exist only because the four
operations forming $D\!\cdot\!A$ and $C\!\cdot\!B$ are reordered away from their
textbook sequence so that each difference or sum feeds its multiplication
immediately, turning one chain into two without altering the dataflow. The three
operations whose producer and consumer register sets do not coincide --- the two
final multiplications and the subtraction $E=AA-BB$ --- are left to reload from the
state structure, and a short tail block, together with one multiplication by the
curve constant performed in C, completes $z_2$ as a seventh chain.

\paragraph{The inversion as one chained block.}
The Fermat inversion is built the same way, as a single inline block in which the
$254$ squarings are grouped into eleven runs of lengths
$1,2,1,5,10,20,10,50,100,50,5$, separated by eleven multiplications that recombine
the intermediate powers. Within a run, only the first squaring loads its operand
from memory and only the last stores its result; between consecutive squarings the
previous output is renamed into the input position by four register moves, the
fifth limb needing no move because the lowest output limb already occupies the
lowest input register. The separating multiplications stand alone, because the
register set a squaring produces does not match the set a multiplication consumes.
This is the same data-movement principle as \Cref{sec:budget}, extended from the
interior of one field operation, where the layer already removes the per-limb
memory traffic, to the seams between operations, where the inlined ladder removes
the reloads.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:c25519-perf}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
The measurements follow the discipline of \Cref{sec:keccak}. The core is pinned to
its base frequency so that the cycle counter and the core clock advance together,
timings are taken within a single process, and each contender is swept over the
twenty-four combinations of compiler and optimisation level, the best result for
each being retained, in the manner of the SUPERCOP autotuner. Since the comparison
is made at the level of a complete scalar multiplication, the aggregate figure for
a backend is the geometric mean of its per-configuration ratios against the
microcode.

\paragraph{The field operation in isolation.}
Timed on their own in a tight loop, the microcode field operations are faster than
the fiat-crypto reference. The squaring completes in roughly 37 cycles, about
$26\%$ faster than fiat, and the multiplication in roughly 58 cycles, about $3\%$
faster. The asymmetry is intrinsic to the kernels: squaring issues fifteen
products against multiplication's twenty-five, so it has proportionally more slack
into which the carry work can be packed, whereas the multiplication is closer to
multiplier-throughput bound and the layer's carry-concurrency advantage has less
room to act. Because a ladder and a Fermat inversion are both squaring-dominated,
the larger squaring advantage is what propagates into the end-to-end result.

\paragraph{End to end.}
\Cref{tab:c25519-e2e} reports the best per-contender result across the sweep, in
median cycles per scalar multiplication. The inlined microcode ladder completes in
about $300{,}000$ cycles and is the fastest of every $5\times51$ contender we
measured: against the field backends it is a geometric mean of roughly $1.25\times$
faster than fiat-crypto, $1.30\times$ faster than hand-written
\texttt{\_\_uint128\_t} C, $1.28\times$ faster than the CryptOpt superoptimiser,
and $1.31\times$ faster than \texttt{donna\_c64}, and it is also faster than the
hand-written \texttt{amd64-51} assembly stack. The field-operation advantage is
not an artefact of our own ladder: when the microcode field operations are carried
into the Bernstein--Schwabe \texttt{amd64-51} framework, that hybrid is a geometric
mean of about $1.08\times$ faster than the same framework running its authors' own
assembly field operations, so the advantage survives transplantation into an
independently engineered scalar multiplication.

\paragraph{Where the advantage ends.}
The fastest scalar multiplication on this core is nonetheless not ours. It is the
Bernstein--Schwabe \texttt{amd64-64} implementation, which uses the saturated
$4\times64$ representation in radix $2^{64}$ and completes in $271{,}326$ cycles,
roughly $11\%$ ahead of the inlined microcode ladder at about $300{,}000$. More
revealing for the layer is what happens when we encode the field operations
themselves in that $4\times64$ representation: the microcode port runs in
$511{,}017$ cycles, a geometric mean of about $1.77\times$ slower than our
$5\times51$ microcode and nearly twice the cost of the native $4\times64$ assembly.
The cause is exactly the carry structure of \Cref{sec:c25519-field}. A saturated
$4\times64$ multiplication produces long, full-width carry chains across 64-bit
limbs, and the add-with-carry instruction those chains want reads the architectural
flag domain that, as recorded in \Cref{sec:findings}, is frozen on entry to a
patch and cannot be threaded through a sequence of dependent additions inside the
microcode. The four-deep saturated carry must therefore be emulated by serial
\texttt{SETCC} operations, which reintroduces precisely the serialisation that the
$5\times51$ kernel's three parallel chains were built to avoid. The representation
that suits the native core best is thus the one that suits the layer worst, and the
representation at which the layer excels does not yield the fastest algorithm
overall.

\paragraph{Summary.}
At the level at which the paper stakes its claim --- the field operation, with the
ladder, inversion, and conditional swap held in common --- the microcode is the
fastest Curve25519 field backend we measured on this core, ahead of an optimising
compiler, a formally verified generator, a cryptographic superoptimiser, and expert
hand assembly, and the advantage carries through both our own scalar multiplication
and the Bernstein--Schwabe framework. The result has the same shape as the Keccak
result of \Cref{sec:keccak}: it comes from a structural per-operation capability
the instruction set cannot reach, here the fusion of multiply, accumulate, and
carry capture into single triads together with three concurrent \texttt{SETCC}
carry chains. And as with Keccak the limit is stated without softening. The fastest
X25519 on this core belongs to a different number representation, the saturated
$4\times64$ form, and that representation is exactly where the one structural
weakness of the layer --- long carry chains under a frozen single-flag model ---
is laid bare.
