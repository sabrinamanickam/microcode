\section{Curve25519 in Microcode}
\label{sec:curve25519}

The second worked example instantiates the methodology of
\Cref{sec:ucode-layer} on the field arithmetic that underlies X25519.
Whereas the Keccak permutation of \Cref{sec:keccak} was realised as a single
large, control-flow-heavy patch that is looped in place, a Curve25519 field
operation has the opposite character in every respect that matters to the
layer. It is a small, straight-line, multiplication-dominated kernel, and it is
invoked many hundreds of times within a single scalar multiplication rather
than once. The primitive that we encode in microcode is consequently the field
multiplication and the field squaring modulo the prime $2^{255}-19$, and the
question that the section addresses is whether a microcode patch can evaluate
these two operations more quickly than the best native code available on the
platform. We interpret the phrase ``best available native code'' deliberately
broadly, so that the comparison is made not against a single reference but
against four qualitatively distinct classes of competitor: the output of a
modern optimising compiler, an automatically generated and formally verified
arithmetic backend, the output of a dedicated cryptographic superoptimiser, and
expert hand-written assembly produced by the designers of the primitive
themselves.

\paragraph{The structural advantage.}
In this setting the advantage of the microcode layer is not register residency,
which was the lever exploited by Keccak, because the five limbs of a field
element occupy only a small fraction of the architectural register file and a
native implementation is under no pressure to spill them. The advantage is
located instead in the carry structure of reduced multiplication. The
performance-critical path of such a multiplication is a sum of partial products
that is accumulated into a small number of running totals and is then resolved
into the canonical limb representation by the propagation of carries between
limbs, and it is an empirical fact about this kernel on a two-issue in-order
core that the limiting resource is the carry propagation rather than the
multiplication itself. The reason is architectural. The x86-64 instruction set
exposes a single carry flag, and multi-word carry propagation is therefore
obliged to serialise through the add-with-carry instruction, \texttt{ADC},
because each such instruction both reads and writes the one shared flag. A
two-arithmetic-unit in-order pipeline cannot overlap this strictly serial
dependent chain with the independent multiplications that feed it, and the
multiplier throughput that the core nominally offers is left unrealised. The
microcode layer removes this obstruction by two mechanisms, both of which are
consequences of the micro-architectural properties established empirically in
\Cref{sec:findings}. First, a single triad fuses a multiplication, an
accumulation of a partial product, and the capture of the carry that the
accumulation produces into one issue bundle, so that the carry capture costs
nothing beyond the multiplication it accompanies. Second, the per-register
\texttt{SETCC} flag domains permit several carry chains to advance concurrently,
each writing its own private flag, without any of them contending for the one
architectural carry flag. This concurrency of carry propagation is, for field
arithmetic, the exact counterpart of the register residency that Keccak
exploited: in both cases the layer performs work that the instruction set
architecture is structurally unable to express, and in both cases that work is
precisely the bottleneck of the native implementation.

\subsection{Representation and Operations}
\label{sec:c25519-rep}

We adopt the unsaturated radix-$2^{51}$ representation, in which a field element
is stored as five 64-bit machine words, termed limbs, each of which carries only
51 significant bits and is therefore free of the upper 13 bits. This is the
representation emitted by fiat-crypto and the representation used by the
Bernstein--Schwabe \texttt{amd64-51} implementation, and adopting it allows the
microcode backend to be substituted for those native backends without any change
to the surrounding scalar multiplication. A multiplication of two field elements
is the $5\times5$ schoolbook expansion into twenty-five partial products, and
the modular reduction implied by the congruence $2^{255}\equiv 19 \pmod{2^{255}-19}$
is folded directly into the cross terms rather than being deferred to a separate
pass, so that the limbs of the product are obtained as
\[
c_i \;=\; \sum_{j\le i} a_{\,i-j}\,b_j \;+\; 19\!\!\sum_{j>i} a_{\,5+i-j}\,b_j ,
\qquad 0 \le i < 5 .
\]
The terms of the second sum are the wrapped products, whose indices exceed the
limb count and which are therefore multiplied by the folding constant $19$ to
account for the reduction. Squaring is treated as a distinct operation rather
than as a multiplication of an element by itself, because the symmetry
$a_i a_j = a_j a_i$ collapses the twenty-five products of the general
multiplication to the fifteen distinct products of a square, with the off
diagonal products entering with a doubled coefficient. The doublings $2a_i$ that
this coefficient requires, and the folds $19a_i$ that the reduction requires, are
not recomputed inside the kernel; they are materialised once by the caller, by
means of address-generation and integer-multiply instructions on the native
side, and are delivered to the patch in registers, so that the microcode is
presented with operands that are already in the form its partial products
consume.

The carries that arise during accumulation are, by construction, both short and
mutually independent, and this is the property of the representation that makes
it congenial to the layer. Because every limb is reduced to 51 bits, the value
that must be carried from one limb into the next occupies at most the
complementary 13 bits, which together with the 51-bit limb exactly fill a 64-bit
word. A carry is therefore a narrow quantity that is propagated by a fixed
sequence of shifts rather than a full-width addition, and the carry out of one
limb does not depend on a carry deep within another. We return to the
significance of this brevity and independence in \Cref{sec:c25519-perf}, because
it is exactly the point at which the saturated $4\times64$ representation, whose
carries are full-width and serially dependent, behaves in the opposite manner
and where the comparative advantage of the layer is consequently reversed.

\subsection{The Patch: Fused Multiply--Accumulate and Parallel Carry Chains}
\label{sec:c25519-patch}

A complete field multiplication, or a complete field squaring, is carried out by
a single execution of one hooked macro-instruction, an event that we continue to
term a firing. The native ladder requests the operation by issuing that one
instruction, whereupon the corresponding patch evaluates the entire reduced
product within the register file --- which is to say all twenty-five, or in the
squaring case fifteen, partial products, together with the inter-limb carry
propagation and the modular reduction --- and returns control to the native side
with the five result limbs in place. There is in consequence neither any per-limb
looping within the patch nor any repeated round trip between native code and
microcode within a single field operation; the firing is atomic from the point of
view of the caller.

\paragraph{Coexistence of the two patches.}
The multiplication and squaring patches reside in the patch RAM simultaneously,
the multiplication occupying 66 triads from address \texttt{U7c00} and the
squaring occupying 42 triads from \texttt{U7d08}, the latter address being the
former advanced by the 66 triads of the multiplication. Because a single hooked
macro-instruction can be redirected to only one patch entry point, installing
both operations at once requires that each be reached through a distinct match
register that intercepts a different macro-instruction, in the manner described
in \Cref{sec:triads-packing}. We hook the multiplication to \texttt{vmwrite},
whose opcode is \texttt{0x0cd8}, and the squaring to \texttt{vmread}, whose
opcode is \texttt{0x0618}; the native ladder thereby fires a multiplication by
issuing a \texttt{vmwrite} and a squaring by issuing a \texttt{vmread}. These
two instructions are chosen because they are inert in the unprivileged context
in which the ladder runs, so that their architectural effect is irrelevant and
only the redirection to the patch is observed. The combined residency of $66+42$
triads occupies $108$ of the $128$ available, which leaves the budget
comfortably unexhausted and distinguishes this primitive sharply from Keccak.

\paragraph{The kernel and its preparation.}
The caller passes the five limbs of each operand in fixed registers, together,
in the multiplication case, with the four precomputed folds $19b_1,\dots,19b_4$.
A short preparation stage at the head of the multiplication patch materialises
the remaining folds in place by three integer multiplications by $19$ packed
into a single triad, a packing that is licensed by the slot-write rule of
\Cref{sec:findings}, under which the latest write to a shared destination within
a triad prevails. Thereafter the kernel traverses the five output limbs, and for
each of them emits a chain of fused multiply--accumulate triads of the form shown
in \Cref{lst:c25519-mac}. The single defining feature of these triads is that the
double-width product is split at issue: the low half of the product is delivered
into a working register, where it is immediately added into the low accumulator,
while the high half is delivered into a second register, where it will be added
into the high accumulator, and the carry produced by the low addition is captured
into a per-register flag in the third slot. A multiplication, an accumulation, and
a carry capture thus share one issue bundle, and the multiplication of the
\emph{next} partial product is begun in the same triad in which the previous one
is accumulated.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={The fused multiply--accumulate--carry
core of the squaring kernel for a single limb. The register \texttt{TMP0} holds
the low accumulator, \texttt{R8} the high accumulator, \texttt{TMP9} the running
sum of accumulation carries, and \texttt{TMP15} the transient \texttt{SETCC}
carry of the most recent accumulation. A double-width product reaches the kernel
as a low half in a working register and a high half in \texttt{RCX}; one triad
accumulates the low half, captures its carry, and folds in the high half of the
previous product, while simultaneously launching the next
product.},label={lst:c25519-mac},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
{ ADD(TMP0,TMP0,RDI),  SETCC(TMP15,TMP0),  ADD(R8,R8,RCX),     NOP_SEQW } // acc low half; capture carry; acc previous high half
{ ZEROEXT(TMP9,TMP15), MUL(RCX,RBX,R13),   ADD(TMP0,TMP0,R13), NOP_SEQW } // seed carry sum; next product; accumulate its low half
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Three concurrent accumulators.}
The decisive structural feature of the kernel is that the sum of partial products
for a single limb is maintained not as one running total but as three independent
running totals, no one of which ever touches the single architectural carry flag.
The low halves of the products are accumulated into the register \texttt{TMP0},
the high halves are accumulated into \texttt{R8}, and the carries that the
individual low-half accumulations emit are summed into \texttt{TMP9}. Each of the
three chains captures its own carry by a \texttt{SETCC} into a private per-register
flag domain, as established in \Cref{sec:findings}, with the consequence that the
carry capture occupies a spare slot of the multiplication's triad rather than
serialising behind it on a shared flag. The arrangement is the exact realisation,
in the field-arithmetic setting, of the abstract claim made in the introduction to
this section, namely that the layer can advance several carry chains at once where
the instruction set can advance only one.

\paragraph{Resolving and linking the carries.}
The three accumulators are reconciled into one canonical limb, and a carry into the
following limb, at the close of each limb by the short sequence shown in
\Cref{lst:c25519-carry}. The accumulation-carry sum \texttt{TMP9} is first folded
into the high accumulator, so that \texttt{R8} represents the entire contribution
of weight $2^{64}$ and above and \texttt{TMP0} represents the contribution below
$2^{64}$. The full accumulated value of the limb is then $S = \texttt{TMP0} +
2^{64}\,\texttt{R8}$, from which the output limb is the residue $S \bmod 2^{51}$
and the carry into the next limb is the quotient $\lfloor S / 2^{51}\rfloor$.
Because $2^{64} = 2^{51}\cdot 2^{13}$, the quotient separates exactly into a low
contribution $\lfloor \texttt{TMP0}/2^{51}\rfloor$, obtained by a right shift of
\texttt{TMP0} by 51 and occupying at most 13 bits, and a high contribution
$2^{13}\,\texttt{R8}$, obtained by a left shift of \texttt{R8} by 13 and having its
low 13 bits necessarily zero. The two contributions are therefore disjoint in
their bit positions, and the seemingly additive combination
$\lfloor S/2^{51}\rfloor$ is realised by a single bitwise \texttt{OR} rather than
an addition; this \texttt{OR} is the operation that seeds the low accumulator of
the following limb, and it constitutes the entire inter-limb link. The output limb
itself, the residue $S \bmod 2^{51} = \texttt{TMP0} \bmod 2^{51}$, is produced by
the idiom of a left shift by 13 followed by a right shift by 13, which clears the
upper 13 bits of the word without a mask constant. The final limb's carry is folded
back into the lowest limb through one further multiplication by the constant $19$,
which completes the reduction modulo $2^{255}-19$.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C,caption={Resolution of one limb and the link into the
next, shown schematically; the exact register names of the output and the
next-limb operands vary from limb to limb. The carry into the next limb is split
into a 13-bit low part \texttt{TMP8} and a left-shifted high part \texttt{TMP1}
whose bit positions are disjoint, so that the link is a bitwise \texttt{OR} rather
than an addition.},label={lst:c25519-carry},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]
{ ADD(R8,R8,TMP9),    SHR(TMP8,TMP0,51),  SHL(TMP6,TMP0,13), NOP_SEQW } // fold carry sum into high acc; low carry; mask-prep
{ SHR(out_i,TMP6,13), SHL(TMP1,R8,13),    ...,               NOP_SEQW } // out_i = TMP0 mod 2^51; high carry = R8 << 13
// ... open the next limb ...
{ OR(TMP0,TMP8,TMP1), MUL(RCX,a,b),       ADD(TMP0,TMP0,b),  NOP_SEQW } // TMP0 := low carry | high carry (bit-disjoint)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Authoring and verification.}
In contrast to the Keccak permutation, whose round body is emitted by a generator,
the Curve25519 field patches are scheduled by hand. The kernels are small enough,
at a few tens of triads, to lay out manually, and the dense and irregular
interleaving of multiplications and carries leaves little scope for a generic
packer to improve upon a careful hand schedule. They are nonetheless held to the
same discipline of slot allocation and hazard avoidance described in
\Cref{sec:triads-packing}. Each patch is verified against the fiat-crypto
reference implementation, which is also the backend that the superoptimiser
targets, over a large corpus of random inputs and a thousand-iteration squaring
chain, and the assembled X25519 is checked against the known-answer test vectors
of RFC~7748 before any performance claim is made of it.

\subsection{Triad Budget}
\label{sec:c25519-budget}

The squaring kernel compiles to 42 triads and the multiplication kernel to 66
triads, and both therefore lie comfortably within the 128-triad capacity of the
patch RAM established in \Cref{sec:budget}. This is the qualitative respect in
which Curve25519 differs most sharply from Keccak. For Keccak the patch budget was
the binding constraint and the governing question was how to make a single round
body fit at all; for Curve25519 the budget is slack and the governing design
variable is instead the density of the packing, because every
multiply--accumulate--carry sequence that is folded into one triad shortens both
the static length of the patch and the dynamic length of its critical path. The
optimisation history of these kernels consists almost entirely of such merges. The
multiplication, for instance, was reduced from 73 triads to 66 by two
transformations alone: packing the three preparatory folds into a single triad, as
described above, and drawing the per-product carry capture out of dedicated triads
and into the otherwise free third slots of the multiply--accumulate triads. The
squaring was tightened analogously, its final limb being compressed from eight
triads to seven by exploiting a cross-slot read-after-write dependence within a
single triad. In each case the merge is sound only because the intra-triad hazard
behaviour catalogued in \Cref{sec:findings} guarantees that the slots of a triad
observe one another's writes in program order.

\subsection{Integration into X25519}
\label{sec:c25519-integration}

A field multiplication or squaring is invoked several hundred times in the course
of a single scalar multiplication. The computation comprises a 255-step Montgomery
ladder, each step of which performs on the order of ten field operations, followed
by a field inversion that is itself dominated by squarings, the inversion being
computed by Fermat's little theorem as the power $z^{p-2}$. Because each invocation
is realised as a single hooked firing --- a \texttt{vmwrite} for a multiplication
and a \texttt{vmread} for a squaring --- the per-firing dispatch overhead of
approximately four cycles established in \Cref{sec:keccak} is incurred once per
field operation and is amortised against the arithmetic that the firing performs.

\paragraph{The inlined ladder and register chaining.}
Our complete implementation, which is the fastest that we constructed and which we
denote the inlined ladder, realises the entire scalar multiplication as inline
assembly built around these two patches, rather than as portable C that reaches
them through function wrappers. The fifteen field elements that a single ladder
step manipulates are held together in one stack-resident state structure at fixed
byte offsets, and the assembly addresses each of them through a base register that
is pinned to that structure for the duration of the step. The microcode firings are
emitted inline within one large assembly block per ladder step, with the consequence
that the compiler is obliged to spill registers only around the block as a whole and
not at the boundary of every field operation. Within that block, consecutive
operations are chained through registers wherever the dataflow permits: the output
limbs of one operation are left in precisely the registers that the next operation
expects as its inputs, and the reload from memory that would otherwise begin the
second operation is omitted entirely. This co-design of the caller conventions is
deliberate. The addition and subtraction macros are arranged to deposit their five
result limbs in the same register set that the squaring and multiplication patches
read as their first operand, so that an addition feeding a squaring, or a
subtraction feeding a multiplication, becomes a single uninterrupted chain. The
principal assembly block of a ladder step contains six such register chains, of
which the simplest is the addition $A = x_2 + z_2$ feeding directly into the
squaring $AA = A^2$; a further chain appears in the short tail block that completes
the computation of $z_2$. Where the dataflow does not permit chaining --- because
the register set in which a squaring leaves its result does not coincide with the
set a following multiplication requires --- the operands are reloaded, and the
schedule is reordered to expose chains in preference to reloads. The four operations
that compute the products $DA$ and $CB$ are, for this reason, reordered from their
textbook sequence so that the subtraction producing $D$ feeds the multiplication
producing $DA$ and the addition producing $C$ feeds the multiplication producing
$CB$, yielding two independent chains where the natural order would yield only one,
without altering the dataflow of the step.

\paragraph{The inversion chain.}
The Fermat inversion is realised in the same manner, as a single inlined assembly
block within which the long sequence of squarings is chained through registers, so
that across each run of consecutive squarings only the first loads its operand from
memory and only the last stores its result. The addition chain that computes
$z^{p-2}$ decomposes the $254$ squarings into eleven runs of lengths $1$, $2$, $1$,
$5$, $10$, $20$, $10$, $50$, $100$, $50$, and $5$, separated by eleven
multiplications that recombine the partial powers; the runs of length two and above
are squaring chains, and the separating multiplications are standalone because the
register set a squaring produces does not match the set a multiplication consumes.
Within a squaring chain a single squaring's output is renamed into its input
position by four register moves, the fifth limb requiring no move because the lowest
output limb already occupies the lowest input register, after which the next squaring
proceeds with no memory traffic whatsoever.

\paragraph{The principle generalised.}
This arrangement extends the principle of \Cref{sec:budget}, that data movement
rather than arithmetic is the quantity to be minimised, from the interior of a single
field operation, where the layer already eliminates the per-limb memory traffic that
register-starved native code incurs, to the boundaries between successive operations,
where the inlined ladder eliminates the reload that would otherwise separate them. It
is the reason that the inlined ladder is measurably faster than the otherwise
identical implementation that invokes the same microcode field operations through
function calls, and it is the integration counterpart of the intra-operation packing
of \Cref{sec:c25519-patch}.

\paragraph{Isolating the field operation.}
The inlined ladder, by its very construction, entangles the cost of the field
operation with the cost of the integration, and so in order to measure the
contribution of the field operation alone we additionally conduct a controlled
comparison in which the ladder, the inversion chain, and the conditional swap are
held fixed and only the field backend is varied. This same-ladder comparison is
conducted against four alternatives: hand-written C using the compiler's native
$128$-bit integer type, the automatically generated and formally verified C produced
by fiat-crypto, the Goldmont-tuned assembly produced by the CryptOpt superoptimiser,
and the hand-written \texttt{amd64-51} assembly of Bernstein and Schwabe. We perform
the comparison both within our own ladder and within the \texttt{amd64-51} ladder, so
that the microcode backend is evaluated against expert assembly not only on a
framework of our own choosing but on the framework of the assembly's own authors,
where the only component that differs between the two measured implementations is the
field arithmetic itself.

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:c25519-perf}

\paragraph{Methodology.}
We follow the measurement discipline of \Cref{sec:keccak} in every respect. Timing is
performed at the pinned base frequency and within a single process, so that the
cycle counter and the core clock advance at the same rate, and we sweep over the
twenty-four combinations of compiler and optimisation level, retaining for each
contender the best result it achieves across the grid, in the same manner as the
SUPERCOP autotuner from which the methodology is borrowed. Because the comparison is
conducted at the level of a complete X25519, the headline figure for the same-ladder
study is the geometric mean of the per-configuration ratios, which collapses the
grid to a single comparable number for each backend.

\paragraph{The field-operation result.}
When the ladder is held fixed, the microcode field operations are the fastest of the
backends we measured. Within our own ladder the microcode is faster than hand-written
C by a factor of $1.24$, faster than fiat-crypto by a factor of $1.20$, and faster
than the CryptOpt superoptimiser by a factor of $1.22$, each expressed as a geometric
mean over the twenty-four configurations and reported in
\Cref{tab:c25519-sameladder}. The microcode therefore outperforms an optimising
compiler, a formally verified automatically generated backend, and a state-of-the-art
cryptographic superoptimiser, with every component of the scalar multiplication other
than the field operation held constant between the two implementations under
comparison. When the microcode is substituted for the hand-written assembly in the
\texttt{amd64-51} framework of Bernstein and Schwabe, where again nothing but the
field arithmetic differs, it remains faster by a factor of $1.07$, so that the
advantage persists even against expert hand-tuned x86 assembly on its authors' own
ladder. The aggregate improvement is governed by the squaring, which in isolation
runs in $37$ cycles and is thereby $26\%$ faster than fiat-crypto, whereas the
multiplication runs in $58$ cycles and is the narrower $3\%$ faster; since a Montgomery
ladder with a Fermat inversion is dominated by squarings, the larger squaring advantage
dominates the combined result.

\paragraph{The integration result.}
The inlined ladder of \Cref{sec:c25519-integration} is the fastest of our own
implementations, completing X25519 in $300{,}197$ cycles, as against $304{,}897$ cycles
for the otherwise identical implementation that invokes the same microcode field
operations through function calls, as reported in \Cref{tab:c25519-e2e}. The register
chaining and the inlining of the firings therefore account for the difference of
approximately $1.5\%$ between the two, a margin that is consistent with the expectation
that store-to-load forwarding on an out-of-order memory subsystem hides most, though
not all, of the inter-operation memory traffic that the function-call implementation
incurs and the inlined ladder removes.

\paragraph{The limits of the result.}
Considered end to end, however, the fastest X25519 on Goldmont is not our own. That
position is held by the Bernstein--Schwabe \texttt{amd64-64} implementation, which
employs the saturated $4\times64$ representation in radix $2^{64}$ and completes in
$271{,}314$ cycles, ahead of our fastest implementation, the inlined microcode ladder,
at approximately $300{,}000$ cycles, as reported in \Cref{tab:c25519-e2e}. A more
searching observation for the layer is that when we port the microcode field operations
themselves to that same $4\times64$ representation, they regress by roughly a factor of
two relative to the native assembly, completing in $510{,}750$ cycles. The explanation
lies precisely in the carry structure discussed in \Cref{sec:c25519-rep}. A saturated
$4\times64$ multiplication requires long, full-width carry chains across 64-bit limbs,
which is the pattern that the layer's flag model handles least well. Since \texttt{ADC}
reads the architectural flag domain that is frozen at patch entry, as recorded in
\Cref{sec:findings}, a four-deep saturated carry cannot be expressed with the
architectural carry flag at all and must instead be emulated by serial \texttt{SETCC}
operations, with the result that the advantage conferred by parallel carry propagation
is forfeited and the kernel reverts to the very serialisation it was designed to escape.
The unsaturated $5\times51$ representation is favourable to the microcode for the
converse reason, that its 13-bit carries remain short and mutually independent and so
admit the three concurrent accumulators of \Cref{sec:c25519-patch}. The representation
that is most favourable to native assembly is consequently the one at which the layer
is weakest, and, symmetrically, the representation at which the layer is strongest is
not the one that yields the fastest scalar multiplication overall.

\paragraph{Summary.}
At the level at which the paper makes its claim, namely the field operation with the
surrounding ladder, inversion, and conditional swap held fixed, the microcode is the
fastest Curve25519 field backend that we measured on Goldmont, surpassing optimised C,
the fiat-crypto autogenerated backend, the CryptOpt superoptimiser, and expert
hand-written assembly. The result has the same character as that of Keccak in
\Cref{sec:keccak}: it derives from a structural per-operation advantage that the
instruction set architecture cannot reach, which in this instance is the fused
multiply--accumulate combined with the parallel \texttt{SETCC} carry chains rather than
the whole-state register residency that Keccak exploited. As with Keccak, the boundary
of the result is stated without embellishment. The end-to-end advantage on this platform
belongs to a different representation, the $4\times64$ saturated form, and that
representation is precisely where the one structural weakness of the layer, the
emulation of long carry chains under a single-flag model, becomes apparent.
